How to Earn CPD Hours for Free with Alison

What CPD is, who needs it, and how to log hours without paying for training.

Read 2000+ words →
+
CPD stands for Continuing Professional Development. In many professions, especially in the UK and Commonwealth countries, you are expected to log a set number of learning hours each year to stay current. Nurses, teachers, engineers, and many other regulated roles track CPD as part of their professional standing. Alison offers many CPD-accredited courses. You can complete the learning for free; the certificate is what you pay for if you need formal proof. Step 1: Check your requirement. Your professional body or employer sets how many hours you need and what counts. Some accept any relevant CPD-accredited learning; others require specific topics. Step 2: Choose relevant courses. CPD is most valuable when it connects to your actual role. A nurse logging infection-control hours is more convincing than random unrelated courses. Step 3: Keep records. Note the course name, provider, date, and hours for each item. Many professionals keep a simple spreadsheet or reflective log alongside their certificates. Step 4: Buy certificates selectively. If your employer or regulator asks for proof, purchase the certificate for the courses you are submitting. Everything else can stay as free learning. Always confirm with your professional body that online CPD from Alison is accepted for your specific requirement before relying on it. Start this course free on Alison →

Is Alison Legit? Accreditation, Scams and Red Flags

How to tell real accreditation from marketing, and how to avoid fake certificate sellers.

Read 2000+ words →
+
Alison is a long-running, well-known free learning platform, and many of its courses are CPD-accredited. That does not mean every certificate carries the same weight everywhere, so it helps to understand what accreditation actually means. What CPD accreditation means: it confirms the course has been reviewed as suitable for continuing professional development. It does not make a course equivalent to a university degree or a government licence. Where it counts: professional development logs, employer training records, and showing initiative on a CV. Where it does not: regulated licences (for example, becoming a licensed electrician or accountant) and roles that legally require a specific degree. Red flags to avoid anywhere online: sites promising "guaranteed jobs" after a short course, pressure to pay before you can see any content, certificates offered without any assessment, and third-party sellers offering Alison certificates at unusual prices outside the official site. Stay safe: always start from the official Alison site or a trusted link, check course details and pricing on the course page itself, and keep your certificate and credential links in one place so you can verify them later. Does a Second Language Actually Help Your Career in 2026?

Where bilingual skills move the needle on hiring, and where they don't.

Read 2000+ words →
+
A second language rarely gets someone hired on its own, but in specific roles it's a genuine differentiator: customer support for international markets, sales teams covering multiple regions, healthcare roles serving multilingual patient populations, and any client-facing role at a company with overseas offices. Where it matters most: roles explicitly listing "bilingual preferred" or "Spanish/Mandarin/French a plus" in the posting. Recruiters treat conversational fluency here as directly relevant, not a soft skill add-on. Where it matters less: highly technical individual-contributor roles with no client contact, where a second language is a nice-to-have that rarely swings a hiring decision either way. How to show it credibly: list your actual conversational level honestly (the CEFR scale — A2, B1, B2 — is more useful to recruiters than "fluent," which most people overstate). Being specific about what you can do in the language ("can handle client calls in Spanish, reading fluency in French") reads as more credible than a vague self-rating. Fastest path to a usable level: consistent short daily practice with a conversation-focused tool beats occasional long study sessions, since language retention depends heavily on frequency. Three months of daily 15-minute AI conversation practice on a platform like Lingzio typically gets a motivated beginner to basic working conversation faster than a once-a-week class alone. If the goal is a specific job requiring a language, target the vocabulary that job actually uses (industry terms, common client phrases) rather than general tourist phrasebook content — that's the gap most generic apps don't close. TRAVEL

Lingzio for Travelers: Getting Conversational Before a Trip

A practical 30-day plan to get conversational before you land.

Read 2000+ words →
+
You do not need fluency for a great trip. You need enough to greet people, order food, ask directions, handle transport, and solve small problems politely. Thirty days of short daily practice is enough to get there for most beginners. Week 1, survival basics: greetings, please and thank you, numbers, and "Do you speak English?". Practise saying them out loud every day, not just reading them. Week 2, food and shopping: ordering, asking for the bill, dietary needs, prices, and sizes. Use Lingzio's AI conversation practice to roleplay a café or market exchange until it feels automatic. Week 3, getting around: directions, tickets, times, and hotel check-in. Roleplay asking for directions and understanding the answer, which is usually the hardest part. Week 4, problems and small talk: what to say when something goes wrong (lost item, wrong order, missed train) plus a few friendly small-talk phrases about where you are from and what you enjoy. Daily routine: 15 minutes is enough. Five minutes of review, ten minutes of speaking practice. Consistency matters far more than long sessions. Before you fly: save your key phrases offline, practise your top ten out loud on the way to the airport, and accept that mistakes are part of the fun. Locals almost always appreciate the effort.
